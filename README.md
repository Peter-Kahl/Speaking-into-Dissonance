## Abstract

An accurate correction is not necessarily an adequate explanation. And an adequate explanation does not by itself mean that the person receiving it can evaluate it. This paper develops a normative account of explanatory authority: authority over where a difficulty is taken to lie, when it may have several sources and when those who receive explanations differ in their capacity to evaluate them. Second-language (L2) speaking is the principal case. When a speaker fails to say what she meant, the difficulty may lie in a slip, in missing knowledge, in the way her first language led her to frame what she meant, in the listener, or in the interaction, and several of these can contribute at once. L2 speaking is not unique in raising this question. It raises it recurrently, for ordinary people, and in a form that can make habitual framing observable. The paper uses an agent-relative concept of epistemic dissonance, an interval in which the location of a difficulty is unsettled for the speaker, to explain why the allocation of explanatory authority matters. That allocation governs whether such intervals can arise, how long they last, and what can be considered within them. The paper distinguishes legitimate closure and delegation from premature closure, foreclosure and abdication. It then proposes a criterion for teaching, listeners and AI language tools. Each attribution of a difficulty should keep within the scope its grounds support. Its grounds should be available for examination. And arrangements over time should develop, and then preserve, the learner's capacity to recognise, evaluate and, where warranted, contest how her difficulties are located. Evidence on the Foreign Language Effect is reviewed as context. It supports effects of reasoning in an L2 but not a lasting effect of learning one. The paper states testable hypotheses for the claims it cannot settle.

## In plain language

You are speaking a foreign language and your sentence comes out wrong. Why? Perhaps you slipped, and you knew the right way to say it. Perhaps you never learned the word. Perhaps the other language simply does not say things the way your own language does, so what you were trying to say does not fit. Or perhaps the listener struggled with your accent and blamed you. Often it is more than one of these at once.

When someone corrects you, whether a teacher, a listener or an app, they also tell you, in effect, what went wrong. Usually they are right, and usually that helps. But sometimes they fix one real problem and leave the impression that it was the only one. Or the fix comes so quickly that you never get to wonder for yourself.

This paper is about who gets to decide where a problem lies, and how to do that fairly. It says three things. Do not claim more than you know. Show your reasons, so the person can check and question them. And let people sometimes try to work out the problem themselves first, so that they learn to judge for themselves. This matters more now that translation and correction apps fix our language instantly, all day.

## What changed from Version 1.0

Version 1.0 (10 September 2025) argued that learning a foreign language trains tolerance of cognitive dissonance, and that this tolerance strengthens pluralist democracy. That argument treated evidence about reasoning *in* a second language as evidence about the lasting effects of *having learned* one. The evidence supports the first but not the second. Version 1.0 also misdescribed several of its empirical sources.

Version 2.0:

- withdraws the civic conclusion of Version 1.0;
- changes the central question to who decides where a difficulty lies, and on what terms;
- adopts a concept of epistemic dissonance that is not defined by discomfort;
- distinguishes linguistic, pragmatic and conceptual mismatch;
- adds sections on accent and credibility, and on AI-mediated language learning;
- states five testable hypotheses;
- lists the corrections to Version 1.0 in Appendix A of the paper.
